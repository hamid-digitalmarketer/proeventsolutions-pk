Designing for Footfall

Positioning comes first: the spot decides how much traffic walks past, and the design decides how much of it stops. A corner spot invites an open, two-sided build; a mid-row spot needs a stronger front face to pull people in from the aisle. The venue assessment happens before the design, not after it, because the mechanic and the location are one decision made together.

Inside the build, queue flow and engagement time are engineered deliberately. Entry and exit are separated so the line keeps moving, and the interaction is sized to the attention the campaign wants — a thirty-second sample or a five-minute experience. Staff choreography is planned with it: who greets, who demonstrates, who resets, and who keeps the aisle side presentable through the day.

Measuring the Day

Organizers reasonably ask what an activation delivered. What can be tracked honestly at a hall activation: visitor counts through the entry point, engagement duration — how long visitors stayed inside the zone — samples or consumables used against stock, and enquiries or lead forms collected by your team.

We agree which numbers matter before the show, not after it, and the floor crew records them through the run. What we do not do is produce percentages after the fact; the figures you receive are the ones the mechanic actually produced, counted on the days it ran.

Numbers only mean something against a target, so the counting plan is fixed when the mechanic is. If the campaign's goal is trials, samples handed over and forms collected carry the story; if the goal is reach, entry counts and dwell time do. Agreeing that goal before the show is what keeps the reporting honest.
